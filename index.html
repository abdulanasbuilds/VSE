Why doesn’t a satellite just fall back to Earth?

It is falling. It is also moving sideways so quickly that Earth’s curved surface keeps falling away beneath it. Explore how changing that sideways speed changes the path.

Gravity is not switched off when a satellite reaches orbit. Without an initial sideways velocity, the path would be very different.

01

Gravity pulls inward

Earth’s gravity continuously accelerates the satellite toward Earth’s centre. “In orbit” does not mean “outside gravity.”

02

Motion carries it sideways

The satellite already has sideways speed. While gravity bends its path, it keeps moving forward instead of dropping straight down.

03

Speed changes the path

At a suitable speed and distance, the path can be a closed orbit. Too little speed can lead to impact; enough speed can let it escape.

Go one level deeper

The mathematics behind the curve

For a circular orbit, gravity supplies the inward acceleration needed to keep changing the direction of the satellite’s velocity.

vcircular = √(μ / r)

Here: r is the distance from Earth’s centre, and μ = GM combines the gravitational constant and Earth’s mass.

g = μ / r²

The slider sets speed as a multiple of circular-orbit speed at the chosen starting distance. It does not represent a specific real mission or altitude.

Evidence and limitations

What this model does—and doesn’t—say

  • It uses Newtonian gravity around one idealized spherical body.
  • It ignores air resistance, Earth’s rotation and uneven mass distribution, the Moon and Sun, and engine burns.
  • The planet’s size, distance and speed are scaled for clarity; do not use this for mission planning.
  • Long bound trajectories and escape paths may be clipped by the edge of the display.
  • Path outcomes are simplified categories for this display, not a professional trajectory analysis.
A visualization can reveal a relationship, but it is not proof that every real orbit behaves exactly like a simplified model.
